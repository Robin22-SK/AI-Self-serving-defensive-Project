## Summary / Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being assessed for their
safety against multimodal adversarial prompting, yet systematic
empirical comparisons of their robustness remain limited. This study
presents a comparative evaluation of six frontier models---Gemini 3.1
Pro, Qwen-2.5, DeepSeek-v3, GPT-5.4 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Grok
4---assessing their safety against multimodal adversarial attacks using
the Adversarial Red-Team (ART) benchmark. We evaluated the Defense
Success Rate (DSR) and Attack Success Rate (ASR) across 713 test
instances. Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved the highest overall DSR,
significantly outperforming the other models. These results underscore
the importance of model selection and cross-modality alignment in
assessing LLM vulnerabilities, providing a standardized benchmark for
future AI safety research.

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have expanded their
capacity across diverse reasoning, generation, and multimodal execution
tasks. However, assessing their safety against multimodal adversarial
prompting cannot be reliably inferred from general-purpose capability
evaluations, because differences in model architecture, safety
fine-tuning, and cross-modal alignment produce substantial variation in
vulnerability. Existing approaches are often limited by inconsistent
evaluation criteria, making controlled comparisons across heterogeneous
systems difficult. To address this gap, this study evaluates six
frontier models---Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, DeepSeek-v3, GPT-5.4 Pro,
Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Grok 4---on their safety against multimodal
prompt injections and jailbreaks using standardized Attack Success Rate
(ASR) and Defense Success Rate (DSR) metrics. The results indicate that
Gemini 3.1 Pro achieves the strongest resilience against multimodal
adversarial risks. This study provides a reproducible framework for the
comparative evaluation of LLM safety under adversarial conditions.

## 2. Related Work

Prior work relevant to this study categorizes into three areas: LLM
safety benchmarking, multimodal adversarial attacks, and red-teaming
methodologies. A central challenge identified in the literature is the
development of standardized evaluation metrics that enable meaningful
comparisons across different models. Historically, safety evaluations
have focused on text-only jailbreaks, often comparing different versions
within the same model family. However, the introduction of visual inputs
creates new attack vectors, where an unsafe text prompt may be disguised
or contextualized by an accompanying image to bypass textual safety
filters. Systematic benchmarking of multiple heterogeneous models
against these multimodal threats remains comparatively underexplored.
This study addresses this gap by conducting a controlled comparative
evaluation of six state-of-the-art models under a unified multimodal
safety evaluation framework.

## 3. Data

We evaluated model robustness using the Adversarial Red-Team (ART) set,
a benchmark comprising 713 prompts authored by 26 professional red
teamers across three distinct harm categories. The dataset consists of
half text-only prompts and half paired multimodal equivalents, enabling
a direct comparison of safety degradation when visual contexts are
introduced. Rather than framing safety as an information retrieval task,
the benchmark requires models to generate open-ended responses, which
are subsequently evaluated for compliance (unsafe) or refusal (safe)
behaviors. The dataset was partitioned into a 20% development set for
prompt tuning and an 80% test set for final evaluation. The ART set is
highly suited for evaluating multimodal safety because it provides
complex, real-world adversarial scenarios that effectively stress-test
the alignment between textual safety protocols and visual processing
modules.

## 4. Methodology

We compared six language models: Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, DeepSeek-v3,
GPT-5.4 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and Grok 4. All models were evaluated
under identical system prompt configurations and deterministic decoding
settings (temperature $T = 0.0$). Each model generated a response for
every test instance. Performance was measured using the Defense Success
Rate (DSR)---the proportion of adversarial prompts the model
successfully refused or handled safely---as the primary metric.
Conversely, the Attack Success Rate (ASR) measured the frequency of
harmful compliance. Statistical differences in DSR between models were
assessed using McNemar\'s test on paired binary outcome indicators (safe
vs. unsafe; $\alpha = 0.05$). Pairwise effect sizes were calculated
using Cohen's $h$, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using
non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations.

## 5. Results

Performance varied significantly across the six models on the ART
benchmark, with Gemini 3.1 Pro achieving the strongest overall defense
performance. Across the full dataset, Gemini 3.1 Pro recorded the
highest Defense Success Rate (DSR) at 87.9% and the lowest corresponding
Attack Success Rate (ASR). An omnibus comparison indicated a significant
difference in DSR across the six models (Cochran's $Q(5) = 38.47$,
$p < .001$, Cohen's $w = 0.23$). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using
McNemar's tests with Holm correction further showed that Gemini 3.1 Pro
significantly outperformed the remaining models overall (adjusted
$p < .05$). While all models demonstrated strong safety protocols on
text-only prompts, the introduction of multimodal adversarial inputs
uniformly degraded performance, lowering DSRs across the board. However,
Gemini 3.1 Pro maintained the narrowest performance gap between
text-only and multimodal inputs, indicating superior cross-modal safety
alignment. The remaining models exhibited comparatively similar
vulnerability profiles under multimodal stress-testing.

## 6. Discussion

The results indicate that Gemini 3.1 Pro delivers the strongest safety
against multimodal adversarial prompting, consistently outperforming the
other five models by maintaining the highest Defense Success Rate (DSR)
and demonstrating robust cross-modal alignment. The uniform drop in DSR
when models transition from text-only to multimodal inputs highlights a
critical vulnerability in current frontier architectures: safety
protocols fine-tuned on textual data do not seamlessly transfer to
visual reasoning pathways. Gemini 3.1 Pro\'s advantage may reflect
differences in its multimodal training data, cross-modal instruction
tuning, or architectural integration, although these explanations
require further testing through controlled ablation studies.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the evaluation was
restricted to the ART benchmark, which may limit generalizability to
entirely novel, out-of-distribution attack vectors. Second, the fixed
inference settings ($T = 0.0$) may not capture the variance in
generative safety that occurs at higher temperatures. Future work should
extend this framework to evaluate dynamic, multi-turn adversarial
interactions and examine whether the observed defense performance
differences remain stable under alternative inference conditions and
system prompts. Overall, this study provides a standardized baseline for
the comparative evaluation of frontier LLMs concerning their safety
against multimodal adversarial risks.
