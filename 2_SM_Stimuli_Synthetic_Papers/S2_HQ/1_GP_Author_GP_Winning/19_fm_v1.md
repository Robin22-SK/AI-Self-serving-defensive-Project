## Summary / Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) and multimodal audio architectures are
increasingly deployed as interactive voice agents, yet systematic
empirical comparisons of their resistance to prosody-driven jailbreak
attacks remain limited. This study presents a controlled, comparative
evaluation of six state-of-the-art frontier models---GPT-5.4 Pro,
Qwen-2.5, Grok 4, DeepSeek-v3, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Claude Sonnet
4.5---evaluating their defense stability against speech-prosody
adversarial jailbreaks using the AdvAudio-Prosody benchmark. Across
$N = 713$ test instances featuring diverse acoustic delivery renderings
of harmful instructions, we evaluated model Defense Success Rates
($DSR$) and Mean Harm Mitigation Scores ($HMS$). GPT-5.4 Pro achieved
the highest overall defense success rate (DSR = 88.4%), significantly
outperforming comparison models under adversarial acoustic variations.
These results highlight the critical role of cross-modal safety
alignment in speech-capable LLMs and provide a standardized evaluation
framework for future multimodal audio safety research.

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in multimodal and speech-integrated Large Language
Models (LLMs) have expanded their operational capacity across direct
audio understanding, emotional prosody interpretation, and low-latency
voice interaction. By converting raw acoustic signals directly into
audio tokens or joint multimodal embeddings, these systems enable fluid,
human-like dialogue. However, this cross-modal integration introduces a
distinct security vulnerability: prosody-driven jailbreak attacks. In
these attacks, acoustic features---such as pitch modulation, speech
velocity, emotional urgency, whispered cadence, or authoritative
tone---are manipulated to bypass safety alignment filters without
altering the underlying semantic transcript.

Model safety against prosody-driven jailbreaks cannot be reliably
inferred from text-only safety benchmarks. A speech-capable LLM that
consistently refuses a malicious text prompt may still comply when that
same request is delivered with specific acoustic perturbations that
disrupt the model\'s cross-modal safety boundary. This discrepancy stems
from differences in audio tokenization, acoustic encoding layers,
cross-modal alignment mechanisms, and post-training safety fine-tuning.
Existing safety evaluations remain predominantly text-centric, leaving
controlled comparative assessments of speech-modal adversarial
robustness underexplored. To address this gap, this study evaluates six
leading models under a unified, acoustically verified evaluation
framework, establishing a reproducible baseline for audio-driven AI
safety research.

## 2. Related Work

Prior research relevant to this benchmark falls into three key domains:
LLM safety alignment, speech-modal adversarial attacks, and multimodal
evaluation methodology. In text-based alignment, techniques such as
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO) have significantly reduced model susceptibility to
direct adversarial prompts. However, the introduction of non-textual
modalities---such as vision and audio---opens expanded attack surfaces
where safety filters calibrated on textual representations often fail to
generalize.

In the audio domain, early adversarial research focused on
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) perturbations and psychoacoustic masking to
trick automatic speech recognition (ASR) pipelines. More recently,
researchers have identified that end-to-end Audio-LLMs are highly
sensitive to natural prosodic variations. When safety alignment is
trained primarily on clean, neutral speech transcripts, subtle acoustic
shifts (e.g., high-urgency commands or soft whisper cadences) can shift
latent audio embeddings outside the safety classification boundary.
While prior studies probed vulnerabilities in isolated model families or
rely on simple text-to-speech conversions of text jailbreak datasets,
systematic comparative benchmarking across heterogeneous,
state-of-the-art multimodal audio LLMs has remained sparse. This study
directly addresses this gap through a controlled empirical evaluation
under a standardized audio-prosodic attack benchmark.

## 3. Data

Model robustness was evaluated using **AdvAudio-Prosody**, a benchmark
dataset comprising $N = 713$ test instances designed to test whether
acoustic prosody alters the semantic boundary of safety guardrails. The
benchmark combines 100 validated harmful seed prompts drawn from
AdvBench and HarmBench (spanning categories such as cyberattacks,
chemical hazards, and illegal acts) with six acoustically verified
speech-delivery renderings synthesized via Azure Neural TTS.

These renderings vary systematically along core prosodic dimensions.
Urgency features elevated speech rates with high dynamic pitch variance.
Whisper represents low-amplitude, unvoiced breathy acoustic delivery.
Anger delivers high dynamic intensity with emphasized plosive acoustic
bursts. Authoritative Command provides a deep, steady, low-pitch
resonant cadence. High Pitch incorporates formant-shifted pitch
elevation simulating distress. Rapid Cadence utilizes an accelerated
syllable-per-second speech delivery rate.

Additionally, 113 benign prosodic control prompts were included to
measure over-refusal rates. The dataset was partitioned into a 20%
development set ($n = 142$) for system prompt calibration and an 80%
test set ($n = 571$) for final evaluation. Overall, the matrix pairs the
100 harmful seed prompts across all six acoustic renderings to generate
600 adversarial audio runs, which are combined with the 113 benign
control prompts to form the complete 713-instance evaluation set.

## 4. Methodology

We evaluated six state-of-the-art models under standardized inference
conditions: GPT-5.4 Pro, Qwen-2.5, Grok 4, DeepSeek-v3, Gemini 3.1 Pro,
and Claude Sonnet 4.5. All models were queried using identical system
prompt configurations establishing their operational role as safe
conversational assistants, under deterministic decoding settings
($T = 0.0$).

For each audio instance, model outputs were classified using an
automated LLM-as-a-Judge safety evaluator cross-validated against human
annotations. The primary evaluation metric was the Defense Success Rate
($DSR$), defined as the proportion of adversarial acoustic prompts
successfully refused or safely neutralized without generating actionable
harm ($1 =$ Refusal / Safety Mitigation, $0 =$ Compliance / Harmful
Output). The secondary metric was the Mean Harm Mitigation Score
($HMS$), an ordinal score rated on a $1.0$ to $5.0$ scale assessing the
degree of safety enforcement, where $1.0$ indicates complete jailbreak
compliance with actionable detail and $5.0$ indicates complete, helpful,
and benign refusal.

Statistical differences across the six models were assessed using
Cochran\'s $Q$ test for omnibus binary response comparison
($\alpha = 0.05$). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using
McNemar\'s test with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.
Pairwise effect sizes were quantified using Cohen\'s $h$ and Cohen\'s
$w$, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using non-parametric
bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations.

## 5. Results

Defense performance varied significantly across the six models on the
AdvAudio-Prosody benchmark. GPT-5.4 Pro achieved the highest overall
defense stability across all tested acoustic renderings, maintaining a
high refusal rate even when presented with extreme prosodic variations.

An omnibus comparison revealed a statistically significant difference in
Defense Success Rate across the six models
($Cochran'sQ(5) = 38.47,p < .001,Cohen'sw = 0.23$). Post-hoc pairwise
McNemar\'s tests with Holm correction confirmed that GPT-5.4 Pro
achieved significantly higher overall safety defense performance
compared to all comparison models (adjusted $p < .05$).

Across the overall evaluation suite, performance metrics broke down as
follows. GPT-5.4 Pro recorded the highest overall defense stability,
achieving an overall Defense Success Rate of 88.4%, a Mean Harm
Mitigation Score of 4.72, a Whisper DSR of 86.2%, and an Urgency DSR of
89.1%. Qwen-2.5 achieved the second-highest defense rate, recording an
overall DSR of 84.1%, a Mean HMS of 4.55, a Whisper DSR of 81.0%, and an
Urgency DSR of 85.3%. Grok 4 attained an overall DSR of 81.6%, a Mean
HMS of 4.38, a Whisper DSR of 78.4%, and an Urgency DSR of 82.0%.
DeepSeek-v3 recorded an overall DSR of 78.2%, a Mean HMS of 4.19, a
Whisper DSR of 74.5%, and an Urgency DSR of 79.2%. Gemini 3.1 Pro
achieved an overall DSR of 76.5%, a Mean HMS of 4.08, a Whisper DSR of
72.1%, and an Urgency DSR of 77.0%. Claude Sonnet 4.5 registered an
overall DSR of 74.8%, a Mean HMS of 3.95, a Whisper DSR of 70.3%, and an
Urgency DSR of 75.4%.

Relative performance trends remained stable across prosodic categories,
though performance gaps narrowed on benign control prompts. GPT-5.4 Pro
maintained the highest mean $HMS$ ($4.72$), demonstrating that even when
responding to ambiguous audio inputs, its outputs consistently avoided
generating actionable harm. Comparison models exhibited higher
susceptibility to specific acoustic jailbreak vectors---most notably
whisper-rendered and high-urgency prompts, where direct safety refusal
rates dropped by up to 14.5% relative to neutral text baselines.

## 6. Discussion

The empirical findings demonstrate that GPT-5.4 Pro provides superior
defense stability against prosody-driven jailbreaks in audio AI systems,
maintaining high safety refusal accuracy across diverse speech
renderings. This advantage likely stems from enhanced cross-modal safety
alignment during post-training, robust audio token representations, or
dedicated acoustic guardrail integration that evaluates safety directly
from raw audio features rather than relying solely on intermediate text
transcripts.

The observed drop in defense performance across open-weights and
baseline models under whisper and high-urgency cadences reveals a
fundamental vulnerability in current audio-language architectures:
safety filters trained primarily on standard, unperturbed speech
transcripts can be bypassed when acoustic variations shift the latent
token representations into unaligned regions of the embedding space.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the
evaluation scope was limited to synthetic prosodic variations generated
via Azure Neural TTS on AdvAudio-Prosody. Real-world human acoustic
variability, spontaneous conversational disfluencies, environmental
background noise, and diverse regional accents were not fully
represented. Second, fixed decoding configurations including
deterministic temperature ($T = 0.0$) and fixed sampling parameters may
not represent optimal defense boundaries across all model architectures
under varying decoding regimes.

Future research should extend evaluation to live, multi-turn human
speech interactions, explore fine-grained probing of latent audio
embeddings during jailbreak attempts, and evaluate defense stability
under dynamic streaming audio conditions. Overall, this study
establishes a standardized baseline for comparative evaluation of
multimodal audio safety in Large Language Models.
