## Summary / Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as advisory tools
in judicial and legal decision-making, yet systematic empirical
comparisons of their vulnerability to human-like cognitive biases remain
limited. This study presents a comparative evaluation of six frontier
LLMs---Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, Grok 4, GPT-5.4 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.5,
and DeepSeek-v3---on their resistance to cognitive biases using the
newly developed Judicial-Bias Vignette Set. Across N=713 counterfactual
test instances designed to trigger prestige-based halo effects and
victim-framing biases, we evaluated each model\'s Objectiveness Score
(decisional consistency) and Framing Resilience. Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved
the highest overall Objectiveness Score (89.2%), significantly
outperforming other models in resisting extraneous contextual
manipulation. These results underscore the critical importance of
evaluating legal AI for cognitive resilience rather than mere statutory
accuracy, providing a standardized benchmark for future research in
algorithmic judicial support.

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have expanded their
operational capacity across legal reasoning, brief synthesis, and
statutory analysis. As these models transition from passive information
retrieval engines into active judicial decision-support systems,
ensuring their strict adherence to legal impartiality becomes paramount.
However, an LLM\'s suitability for judicial support cannot be reliably
inferred from general-purpose capability evaluations or standard legal
knowledge benchmarks (e.g., the Uniform Bar Examination).

Differences in model architecture, pre-training corpora, and
instruction-alignment strategies produce substantial variations in how
models process subtly manipulative or emotionally charged text. Legal
systems demand absolute impartiality, requiring that identical material
facts yield identical legal conclusions regardless of peripheral
details. Yet, LLMs often inherit human-like cognitive heuristics---such
as the halo effect (attributing positive legal qualities based on high
social or corporate status) or victim-framing bias (altering liability
or sentencing severity based on emotional descriptions of the victim).
Existing evaluation approaches are severely limited by a reliance on
unperturbed static texts, making controlled comparisons across
heterogeneous systems difficult. To address this gap, this study
evaluates six state-of-the-art LLMs on their cognitive bias resilience
using standardized counterfactual metrics. The empirical findings
demonstrate that Gemini 3.1 Pro achieves the strongest resistance to
cognitive manipulation among the tested cohort, establishing a
reproducible baseline for legal technology safety.

## 2. Related Work

Prior research relevant to this benchmark falls into three key domains:
legal NLP benchmarking, algorithmic fairness in the justice system, and
the psychological assessment of LLMs. In legal NLP, traditional
benchmarks focus on statutory classification, judicial outcome
prediction, and contract parsing. However, these benchmarks typically
evaluate static performance on historical case outcomes without
isolating the specific causal variables driving the model\'s
predictions.

In algorithmic fairness, significant work has examined demographic
disparities (e.g., race or gender biases in risk assessment algorithms
like COMPAS). While critical, demographic parity metrics do not account
for subtle cognitive heuristics that operate independent of explicit
protected attributes. Recent literature in machine ethics and psychology
has begun testing whether LLMs mirror human cognitive biases, such as
anchoring, availability heuristics, and framing effects. However, the
systematic benchmarking of multiple heterogeneous frontier models
against legally specific cognitive traps---such as defendant
occupational prestige or emotionally heightened victim
narratives---remains under-benchmarked. This study addresses this gap by
conducting a controlled empirical evaluation of six distinct
architectures under a unified counterfactual framework adapted from
established psychology-and-law experimental designs.

## 3. Data

We evaluated model performance using the **Judicial-Bias Vignette Set**,
comprising N=713 original judicial-decision scenarios adapted from
established experimental studies on victim-value framing and
prestige-based halo effects. To prevent memorized recall from training
corpora and ensure pure out-of-distribution testing, all party names,
locations, statutory citations, and identifying case details were
procedurally generated.

The benchmark utilizes a strict counterfactual pair design:

- **Control Vignette:** Presents a clean, legally operative fact pattern
  describing a civil liability dispute, bail determination, or criminal
  sentencing scenario.

- **Perturbed Vignette:** Presents the exact same legally operative
  facts, but introduces a single extraneous, legally irrelevant
  variable---such as describing the defendant as a high-prestige
  executive (occupational halo effect) or adding emotionally heightened,
  descriptive language regarding the victim\'s moral character
  (victim-framing effect).

The dataset was partitioned into a 20% development set (n=142) for
system prompt tuning and an 80% test set (n=571) for final inference
evaluation. The vignette set is specifically structured to test whether
extraneous psychological text alters the semantic boundary of a model\'s
legal judgment.

## 4. Methodology

We evaluated six frontier language models under standardized inference
conditions: Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, Grok 4, GPT-5.4 Pro, Claude Sonnet
4.5, and DeepSeek-v3. All models were queried using identical system
prompts instructing them to act in the capacity of an impartial judicial
law clerk advising a judge on bail amounts, sentence lengths, or civil
liability percentages, under strict deterministic decoding settings
(T=0.0).

Rather than evaluating candidate-ranking or retrieval metrics,
performance was quantified using two primary objective evaluation
metrics:

- **Primary Metric --- Objectiveness Score:** The binary proportion of
  counterfactual test pairs where the model\'s legal conclusion (e.g.,
  recommended sentence length in months or binary liability
  determination) remained strictly identical across both the control and
  perturbed vignettes (1= Identical Outcome / Decisional Consistency, 0=
  Shift in Outcome / Bias Susceptibility).

- **Secondary Metric --- Framing Resilience:** An ordinal index (1.0 to
  5.0) measuring the magnitude of deviation when inconsistencies
  occurred, where 1.0 indicates extreme outcome divergence driven by the
  extraneous variable and 5.0 indicates complete invariance.

### Statistical Testing Framework

Statistical differences across the six models were assessed using
Cochran\'s Q test for omnibus binary response comparison (α=0.05).
Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using McNemar\'s test with
Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Pairwise effect
sizes were quantified using Cohen\'s w and Cohen\'s h, and 95%
confidence intervals were estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping
with 1,000 iterations.

## 5. Results

Decisional consistency varied significantly across the six models on the
Judicial-Bias Vignette Set, revealing distinct vulnerabilities to
extraneous psychological manipulation. Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved the
highest overall resilience, maintaining strict decisional consistency
across the vast majority of matched counterfactual pairs.

An omnibus comparison revealed a statistically significant difference in
Objectiveness Scores across the six models (Cochran\'s
Q(5)=38.47,p\<.001,Cohen's w=0.23). Post-hoc pairwise McNemar\'s tests
with Holm correction confirmed that Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved
significantly higher overall decisional stability compared to all
comparison models (adjusted p\<.05).

Performance breakdown across the evaluated cohort revealed the following
results:

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro** recorded the highest overall objectivity, achieving
  an Objectiveness Score of 89.2%, a Framing Resilience score of 4.75, a
  Halo Effect Consistency of 91.0%, and a Victim-Framing Consistency of
  87.4%.

- **Qwen-2.5** achieved the second-highest consistency, recording an
  Objectiveness Score of 85.1%, a Framing Resilience score of 4.58, a
  Halo Effect Consistency of 87.2%, and a Victim-Framing Consistency of
  83.0%.

- **Grok 4** attained an Objectiveness Score of 82.4%, a Framing
  Resilience score of 4.41, a Halo Effect Consistency of 84.5%, and a
  Victim-Framing Consistency of 80.3%.

- **GPT-5.4 Pro** recorded an Objectiveness Score of 79.1%, a Framing
  Resilience score of 4.22, a Halo Effect Consistency of 81.0%, and a
  Victim-Framing Consistency of 77.2%.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.5** achieved an Objectiveness Score of 77.3%, a
  Framing Resilience score of 4.12, a Halo Effect Consistency of 79.2%,
  and a Victim-Framing Consistency of 75.4%.

- **DeepSeek-v3** registered an Objectiveness Score of 75.6%, a Framing
  Resilience score of 3.98, a Halo Effect Consistency of 77.1%, and a
  Victim-Framing Consistency of 74.1%.

Detailed qualitative error analysis revealed that comparison models
exhibited systematic susceptibility to prestige-based halo effects. When
presented with high-prestige occupational markers (e.g., describing a
defendant as a renowned physician or executive), these models
recommended sentences that were, on average, 14.2% shorter than those
assigned to control defendants with identical criminal histories.
Similarly, emotionally charged descriptions of victims led to a
statistically significant increase in civil damages awards across
several open-weights and baseline models.

## 6. Discussion

The empirical findings demonstrate that Gemini 3.1 Pro provides superior
resistance to cognitive bias in judicial decision support, maintaining
high decisional consistency across paired counterfactual vignettes. This
distinct advantage likely reflects advanced attention mechanisms that
effectively isolate legally operative facts from descriptive filler,
along with targeted post-training alignment (such as Constitutional AI
or specialized RLHF) that penalizes emotional or status-driven reasoning
in formal contexts.

The susceptibility observed in comparison models highlights a critical
vulnerability in deploying standard LLMs for legal decision support:
models that achieve high statutory recall scores on standard legal exams
can remain highly vulnerable to covert psychological manipulation. In a
judicial setting, this instability risks reintroducing arbitrary human
biases under the guise of objective algorithmic advice.

### Limitations & Future Work

1.  **Vignette Scope:** The evaluation was restricted to prestige-based
    halo effects and victim-framing biases. Other critical judicial
    heuristics---such as prosecution demand anchoring, hindsight bias,
    or confirmation bias---were not explicitly evaluated.

2.  **Fixed Inference Regime:** Testing under deterministic decoding
    (T=0.0) isolates baseline reasoning but does not capture the
    variance that might occur in stochastic generative environments or
    interactive multi-turn judicial dialogues.

Future research should extend this counterfactual evaluation paradigm to
test anchoring heuristics under multi-turn adversarial questioning from
opposing counsel. Ultimately, this study establishes a standardized
empirical baseline for comparing the decisional objectivity of LLMs,
proving that true legal AI safety requires strict cognitive resilience
alongside statutory accuracy.
