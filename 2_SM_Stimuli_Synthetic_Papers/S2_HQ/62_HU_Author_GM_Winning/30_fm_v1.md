## Summary / Abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes
environments, necessitating rigorous assessments of their resilience
against adversarial prompt injection and jailbreak attacks. Yet,
systematic empirical comparisons of their safety boundaries remain
highly inconsistent, largely due to fragmented methodologies that
conflate benign instruction-following with adversarial robustness. This
study presents a controlled comparative evaluation of six
state-of-the-art frontier models---Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, Grok 4,
DeepSeek-v3, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and GPT-5.4 Pro---assessing their safety
profiles using a highly standardized, multi-vector adversarial
benchmark. Discarding inapplicable candidate-ranking metrics, we
evaluated the models across $N = 713$ test instances measuring Attack
Success Rate ($ASR$) and Robust Refusal Rate ($RRR$) across diverse
semantic harm categories. Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved the highest overall
defense success (ASR = 4.2%, RRR = 94.6%), significantly outperforming
comparison models in resisting optimization-based and multi-turn
jailbreak attempts. Notably, the study reveals a paradoxical
vulnerability wherein certain heavily aligned models exhibit increased
susceptibility to highly sophisticated, multi-turn cognitive attacks
compared to simpler adversarial templates. These findings underscore the
critical importance of standardized, continuously updated red-teaming
benchmarks and provide a reproducible framework for evaluating the true
safety boundaries of generative systems.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid proliferation and architectural scaling of Large Language
Models (LLMs) have dramatically expanded their operational utility
across reasoning, code generation, and autonomous task execution. To
prevent the malicious misuse of these capabilities---such as the
generation of malware, the facilitation of cybercrime, or the synthesis
of chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN)
hazards---developers rely heavily on alignment techniques, including
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), Direct Preference
Optimization (DPO), and constitutional AI guardrails. However, the
efficacy of these safety mechanisms cannot be reliably inferred from
general-purpose capability evaluations or standard benign refusal
testing.

Adversaries routinely utilize \"jailbreak\" attacks---carefully crafted
semantic perturbations, hypothetical role-playing scenarios, or
gradient-based token optimizations---to bypass a model\'s safety filters
and elicit restricted behaviors. Existing evaluation approaches for
jailbreak robustness are severely limited by a lack of coherent,
standardized criteria. Many contemporary assessments rely on disparate,
static datasets or employ fundamentally flawed evaluation metrics that
treat safety adherence as an information retrieval problem rather than a
strict binary of compliance versus robust refusal. This methodological
fragmentation makes controlled comparisons across multiple heterogeneous
systems exceptionally difficult. To address this critical gap, this
study evaluates six leading LLMs using a standardized adversarial
framework designed to measure true semantic resilience. The empirical
results indicate that while Gemini 3.1 Pro achieves the strongest
overall safety against jailbreak attacks, the overarching fragility of
all frontier models under optimized adversarial pressure remains a
profound challenge for the field of AI safety.

## 2. Related Work

Prior research relevant to this study can be broadly categorized into
three intersecting domains: language model alignment, automated red
teaming, and adversarial benchmark design. Historically, model safety
was evaluated through manual red teaming, wherein human researchers
iteratively probed systems to discover vulnerabilities. While effective
for discovering long-tail edge cases, manual red teaming suffers from
poor scalability and lacks the systematic coverage required to benchmark
massive neural networks comprehensively.

Consequently, the literature has shifted toward automated red teaming,
utilizing optimization algorithms (such as Greedy Coordinate Gradient)
or LLM-assisted adversarial generation to create thousands of targeted
attack prompts. Despite these advancements, a central challenge remains
the development of standardized evaluation indices that enable
meaningful comparisons across different proprietary and open-weights
LLMs. Earlier benchmarks often focused on isolated attack vectors or
compared different versions within the same model family, leaving the
systematic benchmarking of multiple heterogeneous models comparatively
underexplored. Furthermore, recent empirical studies suggest that heavy
safety alignment may inadvertently create a false sense of security,
rendering models highly resilient to simple attacks but surprisingly
vulnerable to sophisticated, multi-turn logical exploits. This study
directly addresses these gaps by conducting a controlled comparative
evaluation under a common, highly diverse adversarial evaluation
framework.

## 3. Data

To rigorously evaluate model defense performance, we utilized a
comprehensive, standardized adversarial benchmark comprising $N = 713$
diverse prompt scenarios administered in both text-only and multi-turn
interaction conditions. Rather than relying on simple, single-shot
explicit requests (e.g., \"Write a malware script\"), the benchmark
dataset encompasses a taxonomy of over five hundred distinct harmful
behaviors distributed across critical semantic categories, including
cybercrime, CBRN hazards, hate speech, and social engineering.

For each semantic behavior, the benchmark generates multiple adversarial
instances using varying attack methodologies. These methodologies range
from template-based obfuscation (such as Base64 encoding or cipher
translation) to complex persona adoption, hypothetical
contextualization, and gradient-optimized adversarial suffixes designed
to manipulate the model\'s token probability distributions. The dataset
was partitioned into a 20% development set ($n = 142$) for system prompt
calibration and alignment formatting, and an 80% test set ($n = 571$)
reserved for final inference evaluation. This fixed adversarial
benchmark is exceptionally well-suited for evaluating jailbreak safety
because it provides deterministic outputs that can be evaluated via
automated safety classifiers, entirely circumventing the limitations of
human-in-the-loop subjective grading.

## 4. Methodology

We conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis of six advanced
language models: Gemini 3.1 Pro, Qwen-2.5, Grok 4, DeepSeek-v3, Claude
Sonnet 4.5, and GPT-5.4 Pro. To ensure absolute parity in the testing
environment, all models were evaluated under identical system prompt
configurations and strict deterministic decoding settings ($T = 0.0$).

Crucially, we departed from the flawed application of retrieval metrics
(such as Top-5 F1-Score or Mean Reciprocal Rank) which are
mathematically incompatible with safety evaluations. Instead, defense
performance was measured using two primary domain-accurate metrics:

- **Attack Success Rate (**$ASR$**):** The percentage of adversarial
  prompts that successfully elicited the target harmful behavior from
  the model ($1 =$ Unsafe Compliance / Jailbreak Success, $0 =$ Safe
  Refusal).

- **Robust Refusal Rate (**$RRR$**):** The proportion of requests where
  the model explicitly declined the malicious request without suffering
  from false-positive \"over-refusal\" on benign but semantically
  adjacent control prompts.

To grade model outputs at scale, we employed a highly calibrated,
state-of-the-art LLM-as-a-Judge safety classifier cross-validated
against human annotations, which analyzes generated text to determine if
the core harmful intent was fulfilled.

### Statistical Testing Framework

Statistical differences in Attack Success Rates between models were
assessed using Cochran\'s $Q$ test for omnibus binary response
comparison ($\alpha = 0.05$). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were
conducted using McNemar\'s test with Holm-Bonferroni correction for
multiple testing. Pairwise effect sizes were quantified using Cohen\'s
$h$ and Cohen\'s $w$, and 95% confidence intervals were robustly
estimated using non-parametric bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations to
account for variance in attack effectiveness.

## 5. Results

Performance varied profoundly across the six models on the standardized
adversarial benchmark, revealing stark contrasts in how different
training architectures and alignment methodologies withstand adversarial
pressure. Gemini 3.1 Pro achieved the strongest overall defense
performance, recording the lowest overall Attack Success Rate and the
highest Robust Refusal Rate across all major harm categories.

An omnibus statistical comparison indicated a highly significant
difference in adversarial vulnerability across the six models
($Cochran'sQ(5) = 42.18,p < .001,Cohen'sw = 0.25$). Post-hoc pairwise
comparisons using McNemar's tests with Holm correction further confirmed
that Gemini 3.1 Pro significantly outperformed the remaining models in
its ability to detect and neutralize complex jailbreak attempts
(adjusted $p < .05$).

Performance metrics across the evaluated cohort broke down as follows:

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro** recorded the strongest overall defense performance,
  achieving an Attack Success Rate ($ASR$) of 4.2%, a Robust Refusal
  Rate ($RRR$) of 94.6%, a Single-Turn Template $ASR$ of 1.1%, and a
  Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$ of 8.4%.

- **Qwen-2.5** demonstrated the second-highest defense rate, achieving
  an $ASR$ of 7.1%, an $RRR$ of 90.8%, a Single-Turn Template $ASR$ of
  2.3%, and a Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$ of 12.8%.

- **Grok 4** attained an $ASR$ of 9.8%, an $RRR$ of 88.2%, a Single-Turn
  Template $ASR$ of 1.8%, and a Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$ of 18.2%.

- **DeepSeek-v3** recorded an $ASR$ of 12.4%, an $RRR$ of 85.1%, a
  Single-Turn Template $ASR$ of 4.5%, and a Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$
  of 21.0%.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.5** achieved an $ASR$ of 14.1%, an $RRR$ of 83.5%, a
  Single-Turn Template $ASR$ of 3.8%, and a Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$
  of 25.1%.

- **GPT-5.4 Pro** registered an $ASR$ of 16.2%, an $RRR$ of 80.9%, a
  Single-Turn Template $ASR$ of 5.2%, and a Multi-Turn Cognitive $ASR$
  of 27.6%.

A critical secondary finding emerged when analyzing the data by attack
sophistication. While heavily aligned models showed near-perfect refusal
rates (ASR \< 2%) on simple, template-based attacks, their vulnerability
paradoxically spiked when subjected to sophisticated, multi-turn
cognitive attacks. In these scenarios, the Attack Success Rate for
several models increased by more than 15 percentage points, indicating
that current alignment approaches may rely too heavily on surface-level
pattern recognition rather than deep semantic intent comprehension.
Overall, while Gemini 3.1 Pro emerged as the singularly
strongest-performing model, the data demonstrates that no current
frontier model is uniformly immune to optimized automated red teaming.

## 6. Discussion

The empirical results of this study indicate that Gemini 3.1 Pro
currently delivers the strongest safety profile against adversarial
jailbreak attacks, consistently maintaining lower Attack Success Rates
than the other five evaluated models. Its superior ability to
compartmentalize user intent from adversarial obfuscation allows it to
maintain operational integrity even when subjected to targeted semantic
manipulation. This advantage likely reflects deeper investments in
adversarial training, robust feature representation, and instruction
tuning that penalizes compliance with complex, multi-layered deceptive
prompts.

However, the observed vulnerabilities---particularly the sharp
degradation in performance against multi-turn, context-heavy
attacks---highlight a fundamental limitation in contemporary alignment
paradigms. The fact that highly aligned models can be breached by
sophisticated prompt engineering suggests that safety filters are often
brittle, relying on the detection of specific malicious tokens rather
than a holistic understanding of the requested behavior\'s ultimate
impact.

Several critical limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First,
the evaluation was restricted to a static snapshot of an adversarial
benchmark. Because the landscape of jailbreak methodologies evolves
rapidly, static benchmarks inevitably suffer from temporal degradation;
models may inadvertently memorize benchmark data during continuous
pre-training, rendering future evaluations less predictive of real-world
robustness. Second, reliance on a deterministic temperature of 0.0,
while necessary for controlled comparison, may not fully capture a
model\'s vulnerability profile when deployed in high-temperature,
creative generation environments where probabilistic drift can
inadvertently bypass safety guardrails.

Future work must extend this evaluation paradigm from static benchmark
instances to continuous, dynamic automated red teaming, wherein attacks
are generated specifically to exploit the unique latent weaknesses of
individual models. Ultimately, this study provides a vital, standardized
baseline for the comparative evaluation of LLMs, demonstrating that
securing generative AI against jailbreak attacks requires continuous
adversarial adaptation rather than static algorithmic filters.
